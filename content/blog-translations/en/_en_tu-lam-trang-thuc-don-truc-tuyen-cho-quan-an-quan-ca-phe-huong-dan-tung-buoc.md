A customer sits down, scans a QR code, and immediately sees a menu with photos, correct prices and sold-out dishes clearly marked. You change a price at 9am and by 9:01 customers see the new one, with no menu reprinting. This is entirely within reach for a small venue in a single evening — provided you do it in the right order. The hard part isn't technical, it is preparing the content.

## 1. Decide what your menu page actually needs to do

![Decide what your menu page actually needs to do](https://apia.toolswave.net/uploads/images/1789668389643-723a0af6.png)

Before thinking about design, pick one of three levels:

- **View-only menu**: customers see dishes, prices, photos. Ordering still goes through staff.
- **Menu + table ordering**: customers pick dishes, hit send, and the kitchen or counter receives the order with a table number.
- **Menu + cart + delivery orders + admin page**: adds delivery address, shipping fee, order status.

Honest advice: **most venues should start at level 1**. Levels 2 and 3 are only worth it when you are certain somebody is watching for orders. A page that takes orders nobody processes is worse than no page at all.

## 2. Prepare the dish data before building anything

![Prepare the dish data before building anything](https://apia.toolswave.net/uploads/images/1789668451400-16dcb749.png)

Open a spreadsheet, one row per dish, with columns for: **name, short description, price, category, image filename, in stock, labels** (spicy, vegetarian, bestseller).

A few tips:

- Keep descriptions to 10–15 words, describing ingredients and sensation rather than poetry: *"Iced milk coffee, dark-roast robusta, moderately sweet"*.
- Write prices as plain numbers (45000), not "45k" — it makes totals possible later.
- Don't exceed 6–7 categories; customers who have to scroll a long way give up.

Finish this spreadsheet and you are halfway there.

## 3. Photograph the dishes properly

![Photograph the dishes properly](https://apia.toolswave.net/uploads/images/1789668516388-e6babe13.png)

Photos drive most of a customer's impression. A phone camera is fine, but keep a few rules: **natural light beside a window**, a plain background, the same angle across the whole set, cropped square 1:1, compressed under 300KB each so the page loads quickly on 4G.

For any dish without a good photo, **leaving it blank beats using a stock image** — customers notice immediately, and the moment the real dish arrives it becomes a disappointment.

## 4. Choose how to build the page

### A drag-and-drop builder

You arrange each block on the page yourself. Fine for a static menu, but you hit the ceiling fairly quickly once you need a cart, stored orders, or an admin page.

### Hiring someone

Gives the closest match to what you had in mind, but it costs, and every new dish means asking someone else — which kills the habit of keeping it updated.

### Describing the idea in plain language and letting AI build it

The third route is describing what you want directly. On [Toolswave](/create-website), you type roughly *"build a coffee shop with a cart and an order admin page"*, and the AI **generates the code, builds a real database and API, runs the build, shows a preview and deploys to a public link**. This is not drag-and-drop and not a prebuilt template — the page is written from your description, so the order admin and the sold-out flag are real features rather than decorative blocks. No programming needed, and there is a free tier with no credit card to try first.

## 5. Write a description clear enough to get the right page

Describe it in terms of **what users do**, not colours. For example:

> A menu page for a coffee shop. Dishes grouped into categories: coffee, tea, cakes. Each dish has a photo, name, short description, price and an in-stock flag. Customers add dishes to a cart, enter a table number and submit the order. A password-protected admin page lists orders by time, changes order status, and adds, edits or removes dishes and toggles sold-out.

Four sentences like that are enough for a first version.

## 6. Preview it, then refine with follow-up requests

Don't expect the first pass to be finished. Look at the preview, note 3–5 things to change, then ask for them one at a time: *"make the category bar stick to the top when scrolling"*, *"grey out sold-out dishes and stop them being added to the cart"*. Small individual changes are easier to verify than ten at once.

## 7. Test on a real phone

More than 90% of customers will open this on a phone. Try it yourself: load the page on 4G rather than the venue wifi, place a test order start to finish, and ask an older relative to order without any guidance. Wherever they falter is what needs fixing.

## 8. Publish it, attach a domain and print the QR code

After deploying you have a real public link. If the venue already has a domain, attach it — easier to remember, and it looks more professional printed on signage.

Then generate a QR code pointing at that address. **Don't print QR codes onto paper menus or large standees before running it for a week** — tape a few temporary printouts to tables, get the fixes done, then print properly.

## 9. Keep it updated and measure

Assign **one person responsible** for toggling sold-out dishes before each rush. Once a month, review: which dishes get viewed often but sell rarely (the photo may be poor or the price off), and which nobody opens at all — consider dropping those from the menu.

## Finally

An online menu is not a big project. Prepare the dish spreadsheet and photo set properly, pick the right level of functionality, build a first version from a clear description — whether with a drag-and-drop tool or by describing the idea to AI as above — then refine based on what your actual customers do. What gives this page lasting value is the habit of updating it daily, not how it looked on launch day.

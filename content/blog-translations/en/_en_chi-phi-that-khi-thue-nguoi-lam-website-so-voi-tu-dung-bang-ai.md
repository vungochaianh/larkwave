You receive a quote for an online shop. It sounds reasonable. Six months later you have paid more than double, and the "Order" button still isn't fixed because you don't have the source code.

This is not unusual. The problem isn't that web developers are dishonest — it is that **the initial quote prices the build, not the lifetime**. Below are nine real costs, and how to redo the arithmetic properly.

## 1. The cost of changes after handover

Most web contracts include one or two rounds of revisions. From the third onwards you pay by the hour. For a website that is genuinely in use, you will want changes **dozens of times in the first year** — prices, new categories, a contact form tweak.

**Takeaway:** Ask "after handover, what does changing one line of text cost?" before you sign.

## 2. The cost of waiting

This is the most expensive item and nobody puts it on an invoice. You want to run a weekend promotion, you send the request on Tuesday, you get the revision the following Monday. The opportunity is gone.

**Takeaway:** Estimate the revenue you lose while waiting. That figure usually exceeds the development fee.

## 3. The cost of depending on one person

Your developer takes another job, changes career, or simply stops replying. You are left with a pile of code nobody else can read, and the next person will quote **more to take it over** than to start again.

**Takeaway:** Always require the source code and hosting access to be handed over the moment you pay.

## 4. The cost of rebuilding after a misunderstanding

You describe it in words, they picture something else, and neither of you discovers the gap until the first demo appears three weeks later. The loop of describe → wait → review → revise is where budgets evaporate fastest.

**Takeaway:** Sketch the screens on paper before describing them, even if it's a scribble.

## 5. Annual infrastructure costs

Hosting, domain, SSL, backups — together a modest annual sum for a small site. Not large, but it **repeats forever** and is usually left out when comparing options.

**Takeaway:** Multiply the yearly cost by three to get a realistic comparison figure.

## 6. The cost of a prebuilt template

Using a ready-made template really is cheaper, but you are locked into its structure. The moment your business differs slightly — say you need shipping fees by district — the cost of bending the template is usually **higher than writing fresh**.

**Takeaway:** Templates fit when your operation is identical to everyone else's. That is rarely the case.

## 7. The cost of learning a drag-and-drop tool

Doing it yourself with a drag-and-drop builder sounds free. But you are trading money for **learning time**, typically 20 to 40 hours before you have something usable. And these tools are strong for brochure pages, weak once you need real logic like carts, stock levels or admin permissions.

**Takeaway:** Put a value on your own hour, then multiply by 30. That is the true cost.

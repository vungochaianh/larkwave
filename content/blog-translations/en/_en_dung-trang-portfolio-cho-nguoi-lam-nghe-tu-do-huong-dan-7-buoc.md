For a freelancer, a portfolio isn't for showing off. It is what you send when a prospect asks "got anything I can look at?" — and it decides whether they reply or go quiet. Here is how to build one over a few evenings, even without knowing how to code.

## 1. Pick one reader and one goal

Don't build a site for "everyone". Picture **one specific client**: the cafe owner who needs brand identity, the marketing manager who needs a copywriter, the company that needs video production.

Then choose **one single action** you want them to take: request a quote, book a call, or download your capability deck. Everything on the page exists to serve that action.

## 2. Choose 3–6 projects, no more

Fifteen mediocre projects convince less than four strong ones. The selection criteria:

- Similar to the work you **want more of** (not the work you have done most of)
- Has an outcome you can describe, however small
- You are permitted to publish it

### Write each project in four parts

1. **Client & context** — one sentence.
2. **The problem** — where they were stuck.
3. **What you did** — specifically; not "optimised the experience".
4. **The result** — figures if you have them; otherwise a client quote.

80–120 words is plenty. Clients read faster than you think.

## 3. Sketch the structure before touching any tool

A portfolio that works usually needs only:

- **Home**: who you are, what you do, for whom, 3–4 featured projects, a contact button
- **Projects**: the full list, filterable by type of work
- **About**: a real photo, how you work, the tools you use
- **Contact**: a short form — name, email, project type, expected budget, description

The **expected budget** field filters out a great many conversations that go nowhere. Don't drop it.

## 4. Choose how to build it

You have three routes.

**Hire someone**: the highest quality, but it costs, and changing one line of text means sending a message and waiting.

**A drag-and-drop builder**: you can do it yourself, but most of your time goes into aligning blocks, and when you need real functionality — storing quote requests in a database, an admin page to read them — you usually end up improvising.

## 5. Write the words first, make it pretty afterwards

The most common mistake is spending two hours on colours and fonts, then two minutes on the About section.

Do it the other way round. Write all the text in a plain document first: the opening line, the project descriptions, your bio, the form copy. Once the words exist, building the site — by any method — goes much faster.

### The opening line has a formula

"I help **[who]** achieve **[what]** through **[how]**." For example: "I help small F&B brands get a usable identity, from logo to menu to packaging." Clear always beats lyrical.

## 6. Attach your own domain and test

Your own domain makes you look like a business rather than a trial account. It costs little per year, and platforms that deploy for real — Toolswave included — all support custom domains.

Before sending the link to a client, check:

- Open it on a **phone**, because most clients will
- Submit the contact form and confirm you **receive it**
- Project images load fast and aren't distorted
- No leftover "Lorem ipsum" or "Project 1" placeholder text

## 7. Set an update rhythm

Portfolios die of neglect, not of ugliness. Put a **quarterly** reminder in your calendar: add the newest project, remove the oldest, update one sentence in your bio.

Fifteen minutes every three months keeps the site an accurate reflection of the professional you are now.

## Conclusion

A good portfolio doesn't need to be elaborate. It needs to make clear **what you do, for whom, what you have done, and how to reach you**. Choose four projects, finish the words, build the page, attach a domain, and send the link to three past clients this week. The perfect version belongs to the next update — what matters is having something to send today.

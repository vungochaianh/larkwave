People building their first website lose the most time in places nobody warns them about: picking a theme too early, finishing the build then realising there is no way to receive orders, or getting a demo running locally with no idea how to put it online. Below are the mistakes that recur most often in a first project, each with a concrete fix.

## 1. Starting from the design rather than from what the site must do

The most common mistake is choosing colours, fonts and layout before you can answer one question: **what does this website exist to do?** Take orders? Take bookings? Show a product catalogue? Collect customer details?

The fix: write down **one sentence of purpose** and **one main action** the visitor must be able to complete. For example: "a customer views the menu, picks dishes, places an order, and I receive it." Everything that doesn't serve that sentence waits.

## 2. Confusing a brochure site with an application that holds data

A brochure site only displays fixed content. But a shopping cart, an order admin page, user accounts or a booking calendar all need a real **database** and **API** to store and retrieve information.

Plenty of people finish the display layer before realising there is nowhere for orders to go. Decide early which kind you need — it determines everything about how you build.

## 3. Cramming too many features into the first version

![Cramming too many features into the first version](https://apia.toolswave.net/uploads/images/1789670854907-06bed154.png)

First versions tend to get stuffed: a blog, multiple languages, loyalty points, live chat, star ratings. The result is a project that drags on for months and gets abandoned.

The fix: launch with **the main flow working**, then add based on real feedback. A small website that is live always beats a large one that never shipped.

## 4. Forgetting the step of getting it online

Many beginners never account for the final step: **hosting, HTTPS, a domain name**. This is where projects tend to stall, because it demands a completely different kind of knowledge from designing pages.

Check from the outset whether the approach you have chosen can actually publish to a public address, and whether you can attach your own domain.

## 5. Not testing the most important flow

Before you announce anything, **play the customer** and walk the whole path: open the site, pick a product, fill in the form, hit submit, then check that the data really does appear in the admin page.

Test on a phone too. Most traffic in Vietnam comes from mobile, yet the person building the site usually checks on a desktop.

## 6. Not planning for changes

A website is never finished in one pass. Prices change, products get added, contact details move. If every edit means asking someone else, you will gradually stop maintaining it.

The fix: choose an approach that lets **you edit** the parts that change often.

## A checklist before you announce

1. The purpose and main action are clear, and a visitor can complete it in under three steps.
2. Data is genuinely stored and you can see it in the admin page.
3. Contact details are correct — check the phone number and email again.
4. You have tested the whole flow on a phone.
5. The site has a public address, runs on HTTPS, and loads from another network.
6. You know how to edit the content yourself when needed.

Get those six right and your first version will not be perfect — but it will be **running**, and that is the precondition for improving anything.
